
If you've been thinking about taking the [Hack The Box Certified Penetration Testing Specialist (CPTS)](https://motasem-notes.net/hackthebox-certified-penetration-testing-specialist-cpts-study-notes-pdf/) exam, you've probably asked yourself at least one of these questions:

_"Is the learning path enough?"_

_"How hard is the exam compared to the labs?"_
_"Do I need to know Python? Bash? JavaScript?"_
_"How much real-world experience makes a difference?"_

In today's episode, we're sitting down with a penetration tester who recently passed the CPTS exam and shared his experience with the community. What I liked about his story wasn't that he passed, it was _how_ he passed, and what his journey says about preparing for one of the most respected practical penetration testing certifications available today.

## "The Exam Took Me 24 Hours..."
Let's start with the statistic that immediately caught my attention.

Although the CPTS exam allows candidates several days to complete it, our guest wrapped up the technical portion in roughly twenty-four hours.

Now, before anyone listening thinks that's normal, there's an important piece of context.

He also has **five years of professional penetration testing and application security experience**.

That's a huge advantage. Experience doesn't eliminate rabbit holes, but it teaches you how to recognize them. Even then, he admitted something every penetration tester can relate to.

Around **five hours disappeared chasing the wrong direction.** Then came another **eight hours writing the report.**

I actually like hearing stories like this because they remind people that even experienced testers waste time. The difference isn't avoiding mistakes—it's recovering from them quickly.

## "Experience Changed Everything"

One thing that surprised many people was his certification history. Despite having five years of offensive security experience, he'd only earned the eJPT before attempting CPTS.

No long list of certifications. No alphabet soup after his name. Just years of doing the work. That tells us something important. Certifications don't replace experience. Experience amplifies certifications.

When he was asked whether his background helped, his answer was immediate.
_"My hands-on experience really made a difference."_

That's probably the biggest takeaway from this conversation. The CPTS exam doesn't reward memorization. It rewards methodology.

## Is the CPTS Learning Path Enough?
This question came up repeatedly.

For experienced penetration testers, the official Hack The Box Penetration Tester Path is an excellent preparation resource.

For beginners? Not necessarily. That distinction matters. Our guest didn't discourage newcomers from taking the path. Instead, he encouraged them to build far more hands-on experience alongside it.

Hack The Box machines. TryHackMe. Community labs. Anything that forces you to think independently. That's advice I completely agree with. Learning paths teach concepts. Machines teach decision making.

## The Biggest Misconception About Programming
One listener asked something I hear constantly.

_"Do I need to know Python, Bash, JavaScript, or SQL?"_

The answer was surprisingly refreshing. Programming knowledge certainly helps. But according to someone who has already passed the exam, it's not what determines success.

You don't need to be a software engineer. You need to understand what you're doing. Knowing how to modify a script, automate repetitive tasks, or read exploit code is useful.

But the CPTS exam isn't trying to turn you into a professional developer. It's testing your ability to assess, enumerate, pivot, exploit, and document. That's an important distinction.

## Don't Download Someone Else's Notes
One of my favorite moments came when multiple people asked if he would share his notes.

His answer? No.

Not because he wanted to keep secrets. Because writing your own notes is part of learning. I couldn't agree more. He explained that every topic in his Obsidian knowledge base links back to theory. Windows privileges connect to privilege escalation techniques.

Privilege escalation techniques connect to practical exploitation. Everything is interconnected.

More importantly, writing notes forces him to ask additional questions. Every time he documents something, he discovers another rabbit hole worth exploring. That's exactly how real understanding develops.  They should reflect how _your_ brain solves problems.

## What About the Exam Difficulty?
Naturally, people wanted comparisons.

Was CPTS harder than normal Hack The Box machines?

His answer was balanced. Some machines felt similar to regular HTB labs. Others required much stronger enterprise thinking.

Pivoting, in particular, is something candidates shouldn't ignore. If you're rushing through the "Attacking Enterprise Networks" module because you want to reach the exam faster, you're probably creating problems for your future self.

He also made an interesting observation. He wouldn't classify the exam as "Insane." Most of it sits somewhere between Medium and Hard. But there's an important caveat.

Difficulty is relative. A Hard box for someone with five years of enterprise penetration testing experience might feel completely different to someone who's only completed guided labs.

## Build One Knowledge Base Not Separate Exam Notes

This was another lesson I really liked.

Instead of creating "CPTS Notes," he simply expanded his existing knowledge base.

Windows.
Linux.
Privilege escalation.
Active Directory.
Web attacks.
Everything lived together.

That's a professional way of thinking. Knowledge shouldn't exist inside certification folders. It should exist inside a system you'll continue using years after the exam. The certification eventually expires. Your personal knowledge base doesn't.

## What Beginners Should Really Do
One high school student asked whether someone new to penetration testing could realistically pass CPTS.

The answer wasn't discouraging. It was honest. Yes, eventually. But not by treating the learning path as the finish line. The recommendation was straightforward.

Start learning.
Complete the learning path.
Practice constantly.
Hack The Box.
TryHackMe.
Free labs.
CTFs.
Build repetition.
Build methodology.

Over time, you'll stop wondering what to do next because offensive thinking becomes instinctive. That's exactly how every experienced penetration tester develops.

## Final Thoughts
The biggest lesson I took from this conversation wasn't about CPTS at all. It was about how experienced professionals approach learning. They don't look for shortcuts. They don't collect other people's notes. They don't expect a certification path to magically make them exam-ready.

They practice. They document. They revisit concepts. They expand their knowledge base continuously. And perhaps most importantly, they never stop building hands-on experience.

If you're preparing for CPTS today, don't measure your progress by how quickly you finish modules. Measure it by how confidently you can approach an unfamiliar target without needing someone to tell you what comes next.

That's the skill the certification is really trying to validate. Everything else is simply part of the journey.
